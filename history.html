What each tier has meant, historically

The JET Index boils three market gauges — the yield curve, Shiller PE, and the excess CAPE yield — into one number, then rounds that number to a letter from F (worst) to A (best), with S held in reserve for conditions that have never actually occurred. The letter itself isn't a forecast — it's a snapshot of valuation and macro backdrop — but 49 years of history show each tier carries a distinct, measurable track record for what tends to happen next. This page exists to turn “today's reading is ___” into “here's what that has actually meant historically, and why.”

The same letter can arise for different reasons, though, so the first question worth asking of any reading isn't just “what tier is this” but “which gauge is actually driving it.” A tier caused by an expensive stock market behaves differently than the same tier caused by a flat yield curve or a thin earnings yield — the composite number looks identical, but the underlying story, and the historical precedent, is not.

The big picture, at a glance

Risk tiers (F, E, D) describe conditions historically tied to above-average odds of a correction. Opportunity tiers (C, B, A) describe fairly-to-favorably priced conditions, where the dominant story has been further upside, alongside a real but lower-odds chance of a correction along the way. Moving down the alphabet means the market is progressively more “priced for trouble”; moving up means progressively cheaper and safer — though as the numbers below show, cheap doesn't mean upside is guaranteed, and expensive doesn't mean a crash is imminent.

F Extreme risk, and exceptionally rare

In short: the rarest and most severe reading the index produces — in the only instance on record, a real correction followed every single time.

F only happens when all three gauges are extreme at once: the market overvalued, the curve weak, and earnings yield negative. Any one gauge being bad isn't enough — all three have to line up together, which is hard to produce by accident.

Example: it's happened once — Feb–Sep 2000, right at the dot-com top, with PE above 42 and earnings yield actually negative. Since it's a single episode, there's no sub-pattern to mine; the rarity itself is the signal.

Implications:

  • In the one time it's happened, F produced zero false signals: every F month was followed by a real 10% correction (~5-9 months out) and a real 20% correction (~9-12 months out).
  • With n=8 months from a single episode, that's “this is what the one time looked like,” not a statistical rate.

E High risk

In short: severe — roughly an 8-in-10 historical chance of a 10%+ correction within two years.

Within E, what matters isn't the label but how far below the D boundary the reading sits. A reading barely tipped into E usually means just one gauge softened a little — a wobble, not a trend. A reading sinking deep into E, close to F, usually means multiple gauges are deteriorating together — a broader, more durable kind of weakness.

Example: in 1999, PE and earnings yield were both sinking hard (PE low 40s, earnings yield negative) — a deep E that was really pre-F — and the dot-com crash followed within months. Compare 2024–2026, where PE and the curve are only mildly over the line and earnings yield is still fine — a borderline E that's really pre-D, and no crash has followed.

Implications:

  • At the 10% threshold, false-signal rates are already low regardless of tenure (7-8%), correcting in 11-13 months on average.
  • Depth matters more than time: a deep E (near F) has never missed, correcting in ~15 months; a borderline E (near D) misses ~14% of the time but resolves faster (~8.5 months) when it hits.
  • At the 20% threshold, tenure is what matters instead: a fresh E still misses about 10% of the time even given 3 years, while a sustained E essentially never does, with the correction landing 21-24 months out either way.

D Borderline risk

In short: the most common warning sign — a 60% historical chance of a correction, but the odds swing a lot depending on which gauge is actually driving the reading.

D is the tier where which input is weak matters more than the tier itself, because the two ways into D mean different things economically. If Shiller PE is pulling the score down, that's a valuation problem — price has run ahead of earnings — and valuation problems tend to eventually resolve through a correction. If instead the yield curve and earnings yield are doing the dragging while PE stays reasonable, that reflects interest-rate conditions rather than a mispriced market, and it can persist for years without needing to correct at all.

Example: Dec 2020/Jan 2021 was the first kind — PE alone was rich (~34) while the other two were fine — and the 2022 bear market arrived about 22 months later. Mid-1995 was the second kind — the curve was flat and earnings yield thin, but PE sat at a reasonable 22-25 — and it took over three years to see any real pullback.

Implications:

  • At the 10% threshold: a fresh D is wrong 40% of the time, and a D that's already persisted 3+ months is wrong only 11% — but that gap is mostly a selection effect, not a reason to expect your own wait to pay off. D's long-running episodes are more reliable from the start (28.6% false signal vs 50% for episodes that resolve quickly), and once inside one, waiting even longer doesn't keep helping — false-signal rates climb back up by month 18-24 (11% at month 6, 26% at month 24). “Tenure” is mostly telling you which episode you're in, not rewarding patience within it.
  • At the 20% threshold, tenure stops helping at all (49% fresh, 57% sustained).
  • Which gauge is lagging keeps working at both thresholds: a PE-led D is wrong 0% of the time at the 10% level and only 26% at the 20% level, resolving in 8-17 months; a D led jointly by the yield curve and earnings yield is wrong 53% of the time at 10% and 40% at 20%, taking 24-41 months when it resolves.

C Fair value

In short: fairly valued. A correction happens about a third of the time within two years — more often avoided than not — alongside real average upside.

C spans the whole “fairly priced” range, so where a reading sits inside that range shows how much room is left before the market crosses into stretched territory. Near the B side, there's genuine room left to improve. Near the D side, it's one soft print away from a downgrade — usually a sign the rally itself is running low on fuel.

Example: July 1993 sat comfortably inside C, near the B side, with 158% of upside still ahead. January 2018 sat barely inside C, near the D side, with only 4% left — the “Volmageddon” correction followed within weeks.

Implications:

  • Overall, a C reading carries about 80.5% average upside before the next peak (~46.6 months out), but is followed by a 10%+ correction 36% of the time within 2 years (16% chance of a 20%+ correction within 3 years) — averaging a 23% drawdown and ~15 months to recover.
  • That risk splits by position: a near-B C reading is wrong only 30% of the time, correcting nearly 4 years out on average; a near-D C reading is wrong two-thirds of the time, usually within 2 years.

B Favorable conditions

In short: favorably priced. A correction still happens about half the time within two years, but it tends to be shallow, and the average upside is large.

A B that all three gauges agree on is a different thing from a B produced by averaging one good gauge with one mediocre one. The first means broadly healthy conditions; the second means a market that's good in some ways and merely okay in others, blended into a tier it doesn't fully earn.

Example: the genuine three-way B's (Aug/Oct 2010, Oct 2011, right after the financial crisis) averaged about 160% upside before the next top. The blended B's averaged only about 80%.

Implications:

  • A B reading carries about 117.2% average upside before the next peak (~61.7 months out), and is followed by a 10%+ correction 51% of the time within 2 years (19% chance of 20%+ within 3 years) — a shallower, faster-healing event than C's (18% depth, 9 months to heal).
  • Consensus shows up mainly in upside, not in how often a correction happens: genuine three-way B's carry about twice the remaining upside of blended B's (160% vs 80%), but the unanimous group is only n=6 — too thin to say its correction odds actually differ.

A Excellent conditions

In short: the most favorable conditions on record. Correction risk is real but lower than any other tier, and average upside is the highest of all.

A tells you conditions are excellent right now, but it has no memory of how long they've already been excellent — so the same A reading can mean very different amounts of remaining opportunity. A fresh A describes a market early in a recovery, with most of the next bull run still ahead. A long-sustained A describes a market that's already spent a lot of that runway, even though the number hasn't moved.

Example: March 2009 — literally the bottom of the financial crisis, the month A first lit up — had roughly 280% of upside still ahead of the eventual 2018 peak. By mid-1983, 18 months into an A stretch that began in 1982, only about 100% remained before the market's next real top in 1987.

Implications:

  • An A reading carries about 137.1% average upside before the next peak (~62.3 months out) — the most of any tier — and is still followed by a real 10% correction 37% of the time within 2 years, but only a 4% chance of 20%+ within 3 years, with the mildest corrections of the three opportunity tiers (19% deep, 11 months to heal).
  • A fresh A is actually more likely to hit one of those smaller wobbles along the way (54% vs 26% for a sustained A), even though it's the fresh A that carries by far the most total upside (120% vs 71%) — the early months of a new bull run are bumpier, not calmer, even as they're the most rewarding stretch to be in.

S Unprecedented opportunity

The composite has never actually reached S in this history (1977–present), so there's no mechanism to describe.

Caution: most of the stories above are one or two illustrative episodes, not dozens of independent trials — the mechanism is likely real, but don't treat the exact numbers as precise. The tables above are the full data these stories are drawn from.

Caveats — what this isn't

  • Small samples. 49 years = roughly 22 danger-tier episodes and 59 safe-tier episodes total, not hundreds. Treat every number above as directional, not precise statistics.
  • Not investment advice. This is historical pattern-matching, not a signal to buy, sell, or time entries and exits, and it isn't personalized to anyone's goals, time horizon, or risk tolerance. Nothing here is investment, legal, or tax advice.
  • Correlation, not mechanism. None of this is a causal model — it's historical pattern-matching over one 49-year sample (really more like 4-5 independent full market cycles). It should inform intuition, not substitute for judgment.
